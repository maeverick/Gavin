Subject: Land Law — Customary Law, Ownership of Land
Source: Olohunde v Adeyoju [2000] 10 NWLR (Pt. 676) 562 — Nigerian Supreme Court

CASE NAME: Olohunde v Adeyoju [2000] 10 NWLR (Pt. 676) 562

FACTS:
The parties disputed ownership of land under customary law. The plaintiff claimed the land was family land granted to their family under customary tenure. The defendant also claimed title under customary law. The trial court and Court of Appeal made findings on the evidence of customary ownership. The case reached the Supreme Court on the question of proof of title to land under customary law.

ISSUE:
How is title to land under customary law established in Nigerian courts?

HELD (Supreme Court):
Title to land under Nigerian customary law may be established by proving:
1. Traditional history of how the family came to own the land (first settlement, grant, conquest, or purchase)
2. Acts of ownership and possession over a long period
3. Testimony of independent witnesses with knowledge of the land and its ownership history

KEY PRINCIPLES:

METHODS OF PROVING TITLE TO LAND IN NIGERIA:
The Supreme Court has identified five methods of proving ownership:
1. TRADITIONAL EVIDENCE: Showing how the land was first acquired — through first settlement, deforestation, gift, or purchase from those who had the right to give
2. DOCUMENTS OF TITLE: Title deeds, survey plans, conveyances, certificates of occupancy
3. ACTS OF LONG POSSESSION: Farming, building, payment of tribute, planting of economic crops over many years
4. ACTS OF ENJOYMENT: Giving the land to others, collecting tributes, granting licence to occupy
5. PROOF OF RECENT POSSESSION: Where title is not clear, recent undisturbed possession may support a claim

BURDEN OF PROOF IN LAND CASES:
The plaintiff in a land dispute bears the burden of proving title on the balance of probabilities. The plaintiff must succeed on the strength of their own title, not on the weakness of the defendant's case.

CUSTOMARY LAND TENURE IN NIGERIA:
- Under the Land Use Act 1978, all land in each state is vested in the Governor
- Customary rights of occupancy may be granted by Local Government
- Deemed rights of occupancy: land held under customary law before the Act continues to be held subject to the Act
- The Act does not extinguish existing customary rights — it regulates them

APPLICATION IN NIGERIA:
Olohunde v Adeyoju is cited in Nigerian land law cases involving proof of customary title. Applied in boundary disputes, family land cases, and cases involving long-standing customary possession. The five methods of proving title are standard reference in all Nigerian land law cases.
